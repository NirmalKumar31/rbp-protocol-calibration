When reporting how well a sequence model identifies RBP binding sites, the
composition-only AUROC obtained under the same protocol should be reported alongside the
headline AUROC, and contributions measured under different negative-set protocols should not be
compared. The first costs one additional logistic regression on nineteen features. The second is
a restriction on inference rather than an addition to it, and it is the more consequential of
the two, because a substantial part of cross-study comparison in this literature is of that
kind.

Apparent difficulty is not a proxy for the stringency of an evaluation. Moving to
dinucleotide-matched negatives reduced apparent AUROC in every dataset examined while raising
the measured contribution almost threefold, and the bias-aware protocol, which is well motivated
as a means of removing compositional and accessibility artefacts, produced the easiest
discrimination and the smallest measured contribution of the three. The design of that protocol
is not at fault; what does not follow is the inference from a lower or higher score to how much
a model has learned. Because distinct RBPs occupy compositionally distinct sites, separating one
protein's sites from another's is largely a composition task, and a protocol that poses that task
leaves a sequence model comparatively little to add.

The relation between the composition baseline a protocol leaves and the contribution a model
subsequently shows was strong for composition-matched negatives, in our data and independently
in that of \citet{horlacher2023}, and absent for negatives drawn from the sites of other
proteins, with a residual difference between the two groups surviving adjustment for the
baseline. This suggests that the more useful distinction for a benchmark designer is the kind of
object the negative is rather than the stringency of the matching, and that contributions are
comparable within such a group in a way that they are not between groups. We note, however, that
this structure was recovered from the 4-mer measurement and did not reproduce for either neural
model, so we present it as a feature of that measurement rather than of the quantity.

Protocol dependence can be reduced by fitting a transformation but not by choosing one from
among the standard set. Eight standard reparameterisations reduced the span without closing it,
and the smallest of them, division by the baseline's headroom, is the two-number report
expressed as a single number. An exponent that closes the span does exist, but it does not
transport: the exponent fitted to our three protocols leaves an independent benchmark
essentially where it started, the exponent fitted to that benchmark is 2.4 times ours, and the
exponent differs by a factor of 1.22 between model classes measured on the same data. This is
the sense in which no protocol-free measure of contribution is available. The obstacle is not
that no transformation works but that a transformation which works has to be refitted for each
measurement, which is precisely what a comparable quantity cannot require.

The composition baseline used here stops at order two, which is what a dinucleotide-preserving
shuffle would hold fixed and is a defensible choice, but it is a choice and it carries weight.
Refitting the baseline at order three, adding the 63 trinucleotide frequencies, removed
approximately four fifths of the 4-mer's measured contribution and 87\% of the two-arm contrast,
and on the bias-aware arm the median dataset retained nothing. For a bag-of-$k$-mers model, most
of what is reported as contribution over a composition baseline is one further order of
composition rather than motif recognition or any positional feature. Any study reporting an
increment over a composition baseline is reporting a quantity indexed by the order of that
baseline, and few state which order was used.

Two observations temper this. Protocol dependence is not an artefact of where the baseline
stops: at an order-three baseline the two-arm contrast remained $+0.0067$, $+0.0309$ and
$+0.0545$ for the three model classes, all with intervals excluding zero, and the span across
protocols did not collapse. And the collapse is specific to models whose features are nested
within the span of the baseline. Over a trinucleotide baseline the 4-mer's contribution was
positive in 65 of 94 datasets against 94 of 94 for SpliceBERT, and after accounting for the
reduced headroom that the raised baseline leaves, the 4-mer lost approximately 1.4 times what
SpliceBERT lost. A benchmark intended to separate model classes rather than to rank them by how
much short-range composition they absorb could therefore raise the order of its baseline. That
recommendation carries a circularity that should be stated: trinucleotide counts are linear
aggregates of 4-mer counts, so an order-three baseline is structurally unfavourable to $k$-mer
models, and it separates models by how much of their signal lies outside short-range composition
rather than by capacity as such.

\citet{horlacher2023} established that negative-set construction changes apparent performance
across many RBP prediction methods and proposed the bias-aware alternative. The present work
does not contest that and depends on it, since their released negative sets provide our only
external validation. What we add is a calibration: the quantity that moves is not only apparent
AUROC but the increment over an explicit baseline; it moves in the opposite direction to
apparent difficulty; it moves by a factor of between 3.8 and 7.6 depending on the model class,
which is comparable to or larger than the differences these benchmarks exist to resolve; and no
reparameterisation renders it comparable across benchmarks. The same structural problem is
documented in variant-effect prediction, where the assembly of the evaluation set both inflates
and reorders apparent method performance \citep{grimm2015}, and the sensitivity of genomic
machine-learning evaluations to the definition of the negative class has been noted more broadly
\citep{whalen2022}. Our contribution is to quantify it for one well-defined estimand in one
well-covered assay, and to separate the part that is arithmetic from the part that is not.

\subsection*{Limitations}

The direction of the primary contrast is implied by the design, since GC matching constrains one
of the composition baseline's fifteen degrees of freedom and dinucleotide matching constrains
all fifteen; only its magnitude is informative. The bias-aware arm is not subject to this
argument, constraining none of the fifteen while carrying the highest baseline.

Every magnitude reported here is indexed to a mono- and dinucleotide baseline. The protocol
dependence is not so indexed.

Achieved matching is not exact matching. The GC matcher's operative acceptance bound is 0.15
rather than its nominal 0.05, holding the nominal tolerance for 94.8\% of pairs, and the
dinucleotide matcher reduces composition distance 2.27-fold rather than to zero, so
degrees-of-freedom statements are exact for the design and approximate for the data.

All three model classes were measured under all three protocols, but the study covers two cell
lines and one assay. Whether the calibration holds for other crosslinking protocols or other
organisms is untested. The transform sweep, the baseline attribution and the recommendation test
are reported for all three model classes; the order-three analysis is reported for the 4-mer on
30 size-stratified datasets and for the two neural models on the two composition-matched arms.

Three provenance limitations affect the neural arms. For 20 of 94 datasets in the dinucleotide
arm the retained scores came from a partition that is not chromosome-grouped, and we report the
74 correctly partitioned datasets as primary with the full panel as a sensitivity; the shifts
lie within the interval half-widths. Initialisation was unseeded across the 1880 fold-runs, at a
measured cost of approximately 0.001 on a panel mean against a reported half-width of
approximately 0.008. The convolutional and transformer arms were trained on different
accelerators.

Positive sets differ slightly between arms. Window construction is identical, but a positive is
dropped when its matcher finds no acceptable negative and the matchers fail on different
windows, so the two composition-matched arms' positive sets have a Jaccard similarity with
median 0.9972 and minimum 0.9237 and are identical in 10 of 94 datasets. The negatives are
therefore almost, but not exactly, the only difference between arms.

No significance filter was applied to positives and no expression filter to negatives. Every
called peak is a positive, so the positive set includes weak peaks that a study applying
ENCODE's standard thresholds \citep{vannostrand2020} would exclude. The expression question is
bounded by the control reported above rather than removed. Region labels are 6.1\% to 8.7\%
incorrect on the negative side by our own audit, which affects the region-matching step
asymmetrically.

The verification harness asserts values rather than the provenance of the code that produced
them, and the fold-partition defect above is of exactly the class that this distinction permits:
it was identified by inspection rather than by any of the 649 assertions. Its manuscript audit
checks only values written to three or more decimal places, so quantities written to fewer were
checked manually.

Confidence intervals on a single dataset's contribution are Wald intervals on the DeLong
standard error rather than bootstrap intervals, and the Firth coefficient intervals reported in
the accompanying repository resample rows rather than genes, which is narrower than gene-level
clustering would give.
