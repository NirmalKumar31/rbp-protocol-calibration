Sequence models that predict RNA-binding protein (RBP) occupancy are evaluated almost entirely
by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on held-out windows.
Positive windows are taken from a crosslinking assay, most often eCLIP
\citep{vannostrand2016,vannostrand2020}, but negative windows have to be constructed, and the
construction is usually described in a sentence of the methods. Horlacher \textit{et al.}
\citep{horlacher2023} benchmarked eleven RBP prediction methods across hundreds of CLIP-seq
experiments under two negative-set definitions and showed that the apparent performance of a
method depends on which definition is used. They proposed a bias-aware alternative in which
negatives are drawn from the binding sites of other RBPs rather than from genomic background.

What that leaves open is the size and the direction of the dependence for the quantity a reader
is usually trying to recover. A raw AUROC conflates the signal a model found with the difficulty
of the discrimination it was given, and the two can be varied independently by the person
building the benchmark. Negatives sampled uniformly from the genome differ from crosslinked
positives in base composition, so a classifier that responds to nucleotide frequency alone will
score well without having learned anything sequence-specific. Matching negatives on GC content
is the usual mitigation, but GC content is a single linear functional of the sixteen-dimensional
dinucleotide simplex, so fifteen degrees of freedom of compositional difference remain available
to any model. Analogous circularity has been characterised in variant-effect prediction, where
the assembly of the evaluation set both inflates apparent accuracy and reorders methods
\citep{grimm2015}, and sensitivity of genomic machine-learning evaluations to the definition of
the negative class is recognised more generally \citep{whalen2022}.

A model's incremental contribution over an explicit composition baseline is less exposed to this
than its raw AUROC. We define the nested contribution as the difference between the pooled
out-of-fold AUROC of a logistic model on composition features plus the model's score and that of
the composition features alone, fitted on the same rows and the same folds. This quantity is
what a reader is asking for when they ask what a sequence model adds, and it is what we measure
here.

We assembled 94 ENCODE eCLIP datasets and constructed negatives three ways: matched on GC
content, matched on all sixteen dinucleotide frequencies, and drawn from the binding sites of
other RBPs assayed in the same cell line, following \citet{horlacher2023}. Model, positives,
chromosome-blocked folds and estimator were held fixed across the three arms, and the
composition baseline was refit on each arm's own windows so that no baseline was carried between
designs. Three model classes were measured under all three protocols: a 4-mer logistic
regression, a 7089-parameter convolutional network, and a fully fine-tuned 19.78~M-parameter
nucleotide language model.

Apparent difficulty and measured contribution move in opposite directions. Dinucleotide matching
reduced apparent AUROC in all 94 datasets while approximately tripling the nested contribution,
and the bias-aware protocol produced both the easiest discrimination of the three and the
smallest contribution. The nested contribution spanned 5.4-fold across protocols for the 4-mer
and between 3.8 and 7.6-fold across the three model classes, against 0.019 AUROC separating the
4-mer from the convolutional network on the same panel. Eight standard monotone
reparameterisations reduced the span without closing it, and an exponent fitted to close it did
not transport to an independent benchmark. We conclude with a two-number reporting
recommendation, and with the limits of what it achieves.
