\subsection{Negative-set protocol changes measured contribution more than five-fold}

Across 94 paired datasets the same 4-mer model gave three different accounts of its own
contribution depending only on how the negative windows were built (Table~\ref{tab:three},
Figure~\ref{fig:three}).

Replacing GC-matched with dinucleotide-matched negatives reduced apparent AUROC by 0.1095, in
all 94 datasets (paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-15}$), while raising the nested contribution from
$+0.0265$ to $+0.0663$. The contrast in contribution was $+0.0398$ ($95\%$ CI $+0.0325$ to
$+0.0477$, protein-clustered; positive in 88 of 94 datasets). Evaluated by apparent AUROC the
model appears to have deteriorated; evaluated by contribution it appears to have improved
almost threefold.

The bias-aware protocol did not behave as we predicted. Because both classes are genuine
crosslink sites, we expected composition alone to fall toward 0.5 and the contribution to rise.
Neither occurred: composition alone was the highest of the three arms at 0.8248, apparent AUROC
was the highest at 0.8370, and the contribution was the lowest at $+0.0122$, or 0.53 times the
GC arm (geometric mean over the 83 datasets positive in both arms; the ratio of panel means is
0.46). Distinct RBPs occupy compositionally distinct sites, so discriminating one protein's
sites from another's is itself largely a composition task.

Ranked by composition-only AUROC, by apparent AUROC or by contribution, the three protocols
order identically. Harder discriminations do reveal more of what a model adds; what does not
follow is that a bias-aware protocol is the harder one. Under GC matching, composition alone
exceeded the 4-mer's own AUROC in 29 of 94 datasets, and the contribution was significantly
positive in 80 of 94, falling to 72 of 94 at the design effect measured for these data
(Methods).

The direction of the GC-versus-dinucleotide contrast is implied by the design. The composition
baseline spans the fifteen degrees of freedom of the dinucleotide simplex; GC matching
constrains one linear functional of that space and dinucleotide matching constrains all fifteen,
so the arm that constrains more must leave the baseline weaker. Only the magnitude of that
contrast is informative. The position of the bias-aware arm is not implied by the same argument. That protocol
constrains none of the fifteen degrees of freedom, and its negatives are not
composition-matched in any respect (median absolute GC difference 0.1387, against 0.0297 for
the GC arm). It nonetheless has the highest composition baseline of the three.

Two artefacts were bounded against matched placebos with twenty placebo seeds each. Strand
misassignment, which arises because a matched negative inherits its positive's strand label,
accounted for $-0.0055$ (CI $-0.0089$ to $-0.0022$) of the contrast, leaving 85.4\%. Negatives
drawn from untranscribed loci accounted for $-0.0043$ (CI $-0.0069$ to $-0.0018$), leaving
89.7\%; 40.1\% of negatives lie in untranscribed loci, but this fraction is balanced between
arms to within $1.2\times10^{-5}$ ($p=0.64$), and a confound present equally in both arms cannot
produce a difference between them. GC-matched negatives are 42.8\% sense-strand against 47.4\%
in the dinucleotide arm, higher in 37 of 40 datasets ($p=5.0\times10^{-9}$), so the spurious
directional cue is stronger in the arm with the smaller contribution and acts against the
reported direction.

\subsection{No standard reparameterisation removes the protocol dependence}

AUROC is compressive near unity, so a contribution measured against a baseline of 0.82 is
arithmetically smaller than the same contribution measured against 0.63, and part of the
5.42-fold span is a property of the scale rather than of the protocol (Figure~\ref{fig:scale}).

Across eight monotone reparameterisations of the same 282 protocol-dataset cells the span was
reduced but not eliminated. Somers' $D$ \citep{somers1962}, an affine map of AUROC, returned
5.42 by construction and serves as an internal control. Unbounded links reduced the span in the
order in which they stretch the upper tail: arcsine 3.99, complementary log-log 3.94, binormal
$d'$ 3.18, logit 2.72. The smallest span, 2.00 (CI 1.67 to 2.46), was obtained by dividing the
contribution by the headroom of its own baseline, $1$ minus the composition AUROC. Normalising
by the excess over chance instead increased the span to 18.2, because that denominator varies
with protocol and approaches zero.

A span of 2.00 should not be compared against unity. The ratio of the largest to the smallest of
three sample means is bounded below by one and is upward-biased, so some span is expected under
any sampling. Simulating equal true arm means while preserving the observed between-arm dataset
pairing gave a null distribution with median 1.064 and 95th percentile 1.175, so the residual
span lies outside what sampling alone produces.

Division by headroom is not a distinct coordinate but the $p=1$ member of the family
$g/(1-c)^{p}$, where $g$ is the contribution and $c$ the composition AUROC, and the sweep above
terminates at the member we recommend. Over the family the span falls to 1.005 at $p=1.544$
(Table~\ref{tab:exponent}), so a monotone rescaling that equalises these three protocols does
exist.

That exponent is a property of the benchmark rather than of the quantity. Fitted to the two
negative sets of \citet{horlacher2023} the equalising exponent is 3.649, and our exponent leaves
their benchmark at 2.340 against the 2.381 it started from. A normalisation strong enough to
equalise one benchmark therefore has to be refitted on the next.

Extending the sweep to the other two model classes qualifies the headline figure. The floor over
the eight reparameterisations is 2.00 for the 4-mer, 2.91 for the convolutional network and 1.68
for SpliceBERT: no model class reaches protocol independence among fixed coordinates and all
three exceed the equal-means null, but 2.00 is the 4-mer's floor rather than a general one. The
fitted exponent also performs worse for the neural models, reaching 1.213 for the convolutional
network and 1.124 for SpliceBERT against 1.004 for the 4-mer, and the equalising exponent itself
differs by a factor of 1.22 between model classes measured on the same benchmark (1.544, 1.843,
1.507).

\subsection{The composition baseline accounts for most of the dependence for a $k$-mer model}

Pooled over all 282 protocol-dataset cells the contribution was inversely related to the
composition baseline (Spearman $-0.600$, $p=6\times10^{-29}$), which is to say that most of what
a protocol does is determine how much room the baseline leaves. Three analyses localise this
(Figure~\ref{fig:baseline}).

Regressing contribution on a cubic in the composition baseline over all 282 cells and then
adding protocol indicators, the protocol label contributed 1.0\% of variance given the baseline
and the baseline contributed 11.0\% given the label.

The bias-aware protocol usually raises the baseline relative to GC matching, but lowers it in 27
of 94 datasets, which permits a within-panel test. Where it raised the baseline its contribution
deficit was $-0.0212$ (CI $-0.0269$ to $-0.0157$); where it lowered the baseline the deficit
reversed to $+0.0028$ (CI $-0.0000$ to $+0.0063$). Within datasets, the change in baseline and
the change in contribution were related at Spearman $-0.664$ ($p=3\times10^{-13}$).

Matching on the baseline directly is possible only across datasets, because the dinucleotide
baseline is below the GC baseline in all 94 datasets and the two are therefore perfectly
rank-confounded within a dataset. Under nearest-baseline matching the $+0.0398$ contrast fell to
$-0.0087$ (CI $-0.0265$ to $+0.0122$, $n=41$ pairs). This design is not a clean test of the contrast. The retained pairs are drawn from opposing
tails of their respective distributions: mean baseline 0.6875 against a panel mean of 0.6274
for the dinucleotide members, and 0.6904 against 0.7827 for their GC partners. The 41
comparisons are served by 24 distinct GC partners, one of them reused eleven times, and
proteins are not matched. Nearest-neighbour matching on a covariate
monotonically related to the outcome, with replacement and across proteins, is biased toward the
null here, and the common support for this pair is 0.073 AUROC wide over 33 of 188 cells.

The GC-versus-bias-aware pair overlaps by 0.212 AUROC over 130 of 188 cells, where the same
design is better conditioned. There the bias-aware arm retained a deficit of $-0.0081$ (CI
$-0.0130$ to $-0.0036$) under nearest-baseline matching and $-0.0043$ (CI $-0.0077$ to
$-0.0012$) as a within-dataset intercept at zero baseline shift. Decomposed by pair, the
protocol label contributed 0.05\% of incremental variance for GC versus dinucleotide and 3.40\%
for GC versus bias-aware, so the pooled 1.0\% averages a comparison that is not identified with
one that is.

Within arms, the relation between baseline and contribution was present for composition-matched
negatives and absent otherwise: Spearman $-0.545$ (GC), $-0.462$ (dinucleotide) and $-0.122$
(bias-aware, not significant). This suggests two protocol families rather than three
interchangeable protocols, with a residual of 0.005 to 0.008 AUROC between families.

These analyses do not generalise across model classes (Table~\ref{tab:generalise}). For the
convolutional network the ordering reverses, the protocol label contributing 6.25\% of variance
against the baseline's 2.34\%. For SpliceBERT the within-arm relation is strong in all three
arms, including the bias-aware arm at $-0.568$ where the 4-mer shows $-0.122$, so no two-family
structure is apparent in that measurement. The variance attribution, the within-panel reversal
and the family structure are properties of the 4-mer measurement and should not be read as
properties of the quantity.

\subsection{The span holds for three model classes and does not grow with capacity}

Both neural models were trained on all three arms under the same folds, seed and code path,
with per-window out-of-fold scores retained (Figure~\ref{fig:models},
Table~\ref{tab:threemodels}).

\centering
\caption{Nested contribution under three negative-set protocols for three model classes,
$n=94$ datasets, identical rows and folds. Spans are ratios of panel means with
protein-clustered percentile intervals.}
\label{tab:threemodels}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
model & dinucleotide & GC & bias-aware & span \\
\midrule
4-mer & $+0.0663$ & $+0.0265$ & $+0.0122$ & 5.42 (4.43--6.58) \\
CNN & $+0.0860$ & $+0.0330$ & $+0.0113$ & 7.63 (6.23--9.63) \\
SpliceBERT & $+0.1754$ & $+0.0890$ & $+0.0466$ & 3.76 (3.27--4.34) \\
\midrule
composition alone & 0.6274 & 0.7827 & 0.8248 & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The span was present for every model class and was smallest, at 3.76-fold, for the largest
model. Across all three, the bias-aware protocol yielded the lowest contribution for all three model classes while
carrying the highest composition baseline, so the inversion between apparent difficulty and
measured contribution is not specific to a bag-of-$k$-mers measurement. The two-arm contrast was
$+0.0398$ (CI $+0.0325$ to $+0.0477$) for the 4-mer, $+0.0530$ (CI $+0.0440$ to $+0.0627$) for
the convolutional network and $+0.0864$ (CI $+0.0778$ to $+0.0951$) for SpliceBERT. On the ratio
scale the ordering reverses, with multipliers of 3.08, 3.51 and 2.38 (geometric mean over the 77
datasets positive in both arms for all three models), so the contrast does not increase with
model capacity.

Decomposing the log multiplier over the 262 dataset-by-model cells in which both arms were
positive, RBP identity accounted for 64.8\% of variance, cell line for 0.2\% and model class for
2.8\%. These shares are not directly comparable: a 79-level factor absorbs 29.7\% of the
variance of relabelled data where a three-level factor absorbs 0.8\%. Against a null that
permutes protein labels between datasets while preserving dataset-by-model blocks, the excess
attributable to protein identity was $+7.2$ points ($p=0.003$). A direct test is more
informative than either share: the same protein's log multiplier agreed between cell lines at
$r=0.586$ (Spearman 0.594, $p=0.0001$) over 40 protein-by-model pairs. Cell line was not
detectable, although with fifteen proteins measured in both lines the design has little power.
Model class was small but detectable ($p=0.023$), consistent with SpliceBERT's lower multiplier,
and robust to the censoring induced by requiring both arms positive.

We do not decompose the span into a compression term and a residual protocol effect. That
decomposition requires transplanting a model's discriminability increment across baselines, and
its central assumption, that the increment is invariant to the baseline against which it is
measured, is violated in these data. Across a grid of twelve specifications, 4 of 6 sign tests
held for the 4-mer, 6 of 6 for the convolutional network and 1 of 6 for SpliceBERT; the log-odds
link reverses the sign entirely, which is the behaviour \citet{mood2010} describes for odds
ratios under unobserved heterogeneity. With three arms the residual's sign follows the direction
of transplant, $+0.0452$ carrying an increment from a high-baseline arm to a low-baseline one and
$-0.0259$ in reverse. We report the raw contrast, which requires no transplant, link or
transportability assumption.

Two provenance limitations apply to this subsection. For 20 of the 94 datasets in the
dinucleotide arm the retained neural scores were produced under a partition that is not
chromosome-grouped. Fold sizes were preserved, so count-based checks do not detect it. Up to 23
chromosomes appear in a single fold, and up to 44.5\% of rows have a same-strand neighbour
within 1~kb assigned to a different fold, against zero under the study folds and zero in the GC
and bias-aware arms. The 4-mer is refit on the study folds for every dataset and is unaffected.
Restricted to the 74 correctly partitioned datasets the contrasts were $+0.0436$, $+0.0506$ and
$+0.0878$, all within the protein-clustered interval half-widths of the full-panel values, which
we report as primary for the neural models. Separately, the 4-mer entered the nested fit as a
log odds and the neural models as probabilities; logistic regression is not invariant to a
nonlinear transform of a covariate. Refitting the neural arms on the logit scale increased their
contributions by $+0.0008$ (CNN) and $+0.0030$ (SpliceBERT) on the GC arm and moved the contrasts
by at most 0.0004, so the reported values are conservative. AUROC itself is invariant to monotone
transforms.

\subsection{Reporting the baseline helps in sample but does not replicate out of sample}

Reporting the composition-only AUROC alongside the headline AUROC is useful only if it improves
something measurable. We tested it in the situation it is intended for, a reader holding two
studies that used different protocols and wishing to compare what the models contributed
(Figure~\ref{fig:recommendation}).

On our data, normalising by headroom improved cross-protocol rank agreement in 3 of 3 protocol
pairs and reduced disagreement on a common scale in 3 of 3, by 58\%, 10\% and 48\%. Rank
agreement is scale-free and cannot be flattered by normalisation. This held for all three model
classes, with mean changes in rank agreement of $+0.040$ (4-mer), $+0.094$ (convolutional
network) and $+0.061$ (SpliceBERT).

Two results qualify it. Only the GC-versus-dinucleotide improvement has an interval excluding
zero ($+0.051$, CI $+0.007$ to $+0.115$); the remaining two pairs are consistent in direction
and individually null, and the pair that reaches significance is the one in which the protocol
label contributes 0.05\% of variance. More importantly, the test does not replicate out of
sample. On the 45 datasets of \citet{horlacher2023}, rank agreement fell from 0.706 to 0.656
under normalisation and disagreement rose from 0.860 to 0.908, which are the two criteria we
specified in advance as falsifying. The change in rank agreement was $-0.050$ (CI $-0.222$ to
$+0.140$), so at $n=45$ this is a failure to replicate rather than a refutation.

We therefore recommend reporting the composition-only AUROC obtained under the same protocol
alongside every headline AUROC, on the grounds that it is the only summary of the protocol's
effect that a reader can act on, and we do not recommend treating any normalisation as rendering
contributions comparable across protocols.
